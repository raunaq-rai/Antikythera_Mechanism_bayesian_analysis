\section*{Introduction}

The Antikythera Mechanism is one of the most remarkable technological discoveries from the ancient world. Found in 1901 (by accident) in a shipwreck near the Greek island of Antikythera, the device dates back to between 140 and 60 BCE (perhaps another opportunity for bayesian inference). What makes it so extraordinary is its system of bronze gears, which could be manually turned to display a wide range of astronomical phenomena. Housed in a wooden box, the mechanism likely showed the positions of the Sun and Moon, lunar phases, eclipse predictions, and even events tied to the ancient Greek calendar such as the Olympic Games \cite{Seiradakis2018}.

Thanks to modern imaging techniques like X-ray tomography, researchers have been able to reconstruct parts of the mechanism and get a clearer picture of how it worked. One key component is the calendar ring — a circular disk punctured with holes arranged around its edge. Only part of this ring survives, but it provides a fascinating clue to the type of calendar the device may have used. While it was once thought to represent a 365-day solar calendar, new analysis suggests it may instead follow a 354-day lunar cycle \cite{Budiselic2021}.

In this project, I tackle a Bayesian inference problem related to this calendar ring: how many holes did the full ring originally contain? Using the known positions of 81 holes across eight broken and misaligned fragments, I apply Hamiltonian Monte Carlo to estimate the total number of holes. Two different models are used to account for uncertainties in the hole positions: one assuming isotropic Gaussian error, and another that separates radial and tangential uncertainties \cite{Woan2024}. By comparing the results from both models, I aim to determine which best fits the data and what this can tell us about how the mechanism was designed and about the way time was measured in the ancient world.

\section{Hole Location model}

Following the model described in Section 2 of Woan and Bayley~\cite{Woan2024}, we assume that the original calendar ring contained $N$ holes, evenly spaced around a circle of radius $r$. The full ring no longer survives, and the measured hole locations come from $s = 8$ fragmented sections. Each fragment is slightly misaligned due to relative translations and in-plane rotations.

The Cartesian coordinates of the $i$-th hole in a given section can be modelled as:
\begin{equation}
    \vec{m}_i = 
    \begin{pmatrix}
        x_i \\
        y_i
    \end{pmatrix}
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        r \cos\left( \frac{2\pi(i-1)}{N} + \alpha_j \right) + x_{0j} \\
        r \sin\left( \frac{2\pi(i-1)}{N} + \alpha_j \right) + y_{0j}
    \end{pmatrix},
    \label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
where $(x_{0j}, y_{0j})$ is the translation of section $j$, and $\alpha_j$ is its relative angular offset. The model assumes all fragments lie in the same $x$-$y$ plane and are internally undistorted.


\section{Likelihood Model}

To assess how well a proposed model predicts the observed hole positions, we assume that each measured hole location $\vec{d}_i = (x_i, y_i)$ is an independent realisation from a two-dimensional Gaussian distribution centred on a predicted location $\vec{m}_i = (x_i^{\text{model}}, y_i^{\text{model}})$. The set of all measurements is denoted $D = \{ \vec{d}_i \mid i = 1, \dots, N_{\text{holes}} \}$, with $N_{\text{holes}} = 81$.

\subsection{General Form of the Likelihood}

Assuming independent Gaussian errors, the likelihood for a single hole is:
\begin{equation}
    p(\vec{d}_i \mid \vec{m}_i, \Sigma) = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{|\Sigma|}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} (\vec{d}_i - \vec{m}_i)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\vec{d}_i - \vec{m}_i) \right),
\end{equation}
where $\Sigma$ is the $2 \times 2$ covariance matrix characterising the uncertainty. Aggregating over all holes, the full log-likelihood is:
\begin{equation}
    \log \mathcal{L}(D \mid \theta) = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{holes}}} \left[ (\vec{d}_i - \vec{m}_i)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\vec{d}_i - \vec{m}_i) + \log(2\pi |\Sigma|) \right],
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ denotes the full set of model parameters:
\[
\theta = \left\{ N, r, \text{error parameters in } \Sigma, (x_j, y_j, \alpha_j) \text{ for } j = 0,\dots,7 \right\}.
\]

\subsection{Isotropic Gaussian Error Model}

In the isotropic case, errors in $x$ and $y$ directions are assumed equal and uncorrelated:
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma = \sigma^2 I, \quad \Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} I, \quad |\Sigma| = \sigma^4,
\end{equation}
with a single noise parameter $\sigma$. Substituting into the log-likelihood yields:
\begin{equation}
    \log \mathcal{L}(D \mid \theta) = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{holes}}} \left\| \vec{d}_i - \vec{m}_i \right\|^2 - \frac{N_{\text{holes}}}{2} \log(2\pi \sigma^2).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Anisotropic Radial–Tangential Error Model}

To account for direction-dependent uncertainty, we define a local radial–tangential frame for each hole. Given angular position $\phi_i$, the unit vectors are:
\begin{align}
    \hat{r}_i &= \begin{pmatrix} \cos\phi_i \\ \sin\phi_i \end{pmatrix}, &
    \hat{t}_i &= \begin{pmatrix} -\sin\phi_i \\ \cos\phi_i \end{pmatrix}.
\end{align}
The displacement between predicted and observed positions is:
\begin{equation}
    \vec{\delta}_i = \vec{d}_i - \vec{m}_i.
\end{equation}
Projected onto the local basis:
\begin{align}
    \delta_{r,i} &= \hat{r}_i^\top \vec{\delta}_i, &
    \delta_{t,i} &= \hat{t}_i^\top \vec{\delta}_i.
\end{align}

\subsubsection*{Mahalanobis Distance and Log-Likelihood}

The Mahalanobis distance generalises Euclidean distance by incorporating directional uncertainty:
\begin{equation}
    D_i^2 = \begin{pmatrix} \delta_{r,i} \\ \delta_{t,i} \end{pmatrix}^\top
            \begin{pmatrix} 1/\sigma_r^2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/\sigma_t^2 \end{pmatrix}
            \begin{pmatrix} \delta_{r,i} \\ \delta_{t,i} \end{pmatrix}
          = \frac{\delta_{r,i}^2}{\sigma_r^2} + \frac{\delta_{t,i}^2}{\sigma_t^2}.
\end{equation}
This distance penalises deviations more strongly in directions of higher precision (smaller $\sigma$). It replaces the quadratic form in Equation (2) for the anisotropic case, leading to:
\begin{equation}
    \log \mathcal{L}(D \mid \theta) = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{holes}}} \left[ D_i^2 + \log(2\pi \sigma_r \sigma_t) \right],
\end{equation}
where the covariance determinant is $|\Sigma_{\text{rt}}| = \sigma_r^2 \sigma_t^2$.

\subsection{Parameter Summary}

All parameters influence the likelihood in one of three ways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $N$ and $r$ define the angular placement and radius of holes around the full ring.
    \item $(x_j, y_j, \alpha_j)$ describe the translation and rotation of each fractured segment.
    \item $\sigma$ (isotropic) or $(\sigma_r, \sigma_t)$ (anisotropic) define the scale of measurement uncertainty and influence both the distance and normalisation terms in the log-likelihood.
\end{itemize}

Both error models were implemented in NumPy and JAX. The JAX version supports automatic differentiation and was used for posterior sampling with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo via \texttt{NumPyro}. Consistency between implementations was verified numerically.

\section{Derivatives of Log-Likelihood}

In addition to computing the log-likelihood $\log L(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})$, we computed its gradient with respect to the model parameters $\theta_\mu$:

\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_\mu} \log L(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})
\]

Rather than deriving analytic expressions for the derivatives, we opted to use automatic differentiation via the jax library. The log-likelihood was implemented in a fully JAX-compatible format, which enabled straightforward computation of the gradient and Hessian. Automatic differentiation works by applying the chain rule systematically to the sequence of operations used to evaluate the function, allowing exact derivatives to be computed efficiently and accurately without symbolic algebra or numerical approximation.


To ensure the correctness of the computed gradients, we also implemented a finite difference approximation using central differences:

\[
\frac{\partial \log L}{\partial \theta_\mu} \approx \frac{\log L(\theta_\mu + \epsilon) - \log L(\theta_\mu - \epsilon)}{2\epsilon}
\]

This was implemented using NumPy and compared against the JAX gradients for all parameters. To test the gradient computation independently of model fitting, we evaluated both methods at a set of arbitrary parameter values that we selected manually. This allowed us to verify that the automatic differentiation mechanism worked correctly across a realistic region of parameter space.

We then computed the relative error between the two methods:

\[
\text{Relative Difference} = \frac{|\nabla_{\text{JAX}} - \nabla_{\text{FD}}|}{|\nabla_{\text{FD}}| + \epsilon}
\]

The results were visualised in a bar chart with a logarithmic y-axis, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rel_diff}. The relative differences were found to be negligible (on the order of $10^{-6}$ or less), confirming the accuracy of the automatic differentiation approach.

\begin{figure}[H]

\end{figure}

Given the simplicity and clarity of using \texttt{jax}, as well as the reliability of the results validated via finite differences, we chose to use automatic differentiation for all downstream inference tasks.

We used the \texttt{L-BFGS-B} optimisation algorithm \cite{liu1989lbfgs} to maximise the log-likelihood function, with gradients computed via automatic differentiation using \texttt{jax}. Optimisation was carried out on the negative log-likelihood.

\section{Bayesian Inference and Posterior Sampling}

To refine our understanding of the hole positions, we performed Bayesian inference using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) sampling, implemented via the \texttt{NumPyro} library. This approach allowed us to estimate the full posterior distributions of the model parameters, incorporating both prior knowledge and the observed data.

The priors were informed by maximum likelihood estimates (MLE), previous literature, and physical considerations—particularly the hypothesis that the mechanism tracks a lunar calendar. These priors are summarised below and visualised in Figure~\ref{fig:priors_plot}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Total number of holes:} $N \sim \mathcal{N}(354.37, 3.5)$, centred on the length of a lunar year [\textbf{REF}].
    \item \textbf{Ring radius:} $R \sim \mathcal{N}(77.35, 2)$, based on previous geometric measurements [\textbf{REF}].
    \item \textbf{Uncertainties:}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item For the \textit{anisotropic model}, radial and tangential uncertainties were given log-normal priors:
            \[
            \sigma_r \sim \text{LogNormal}(\log(0.028), 0.25), \quad \sigma_t \sim \text{LogNormal}(\log(0.13), 0.3).
            \]
            \item For the \textit{isotropic model}, the shared uncertainty parameter was:
            \[
            \sigma \sim \text{LogNormal}(\log(0.13), 0.45).
            \]
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Section-specific parameters:} angular offsets $\phi_j$, and spatial offsets $x_j$, $y_j$ were modelled as:
    \[
    \phi_j \sim \mathcal{N}(-2.5, 0.5), \quad x_j \sim \mathcal{N}(80, 5), \quad y_j \sim \mathcal{N}(135, 5).
    \]
\end{itemize}

These priors were designed to reflect both physical constraints and measurement uncertainties, while allowing sufficient flexibility for exploration of the posterior space.

\section{Covariance Structure and Model Comparison}

The covariance matrix $\Sigma$ plays a central role in modelling uncertainty in the measured hole positions. In two dimensions, $\Sigma$ defines the shape and orientation of the uncertainty ellipse around each predicted hole location $\vec{m}_i$, encoding not only the variance in the $x$ and $y$ directions but also potential correlation between them. In the isotropic case, the same uncertainty is assumed in all directions, leading to circular error regions:
\[
\Sigma = \sigma^2 I.
\]

However, this may be overly simplistic. A more physically realistic model accounts for different uncertainty along the radial and tangential directions of the ring:
\[
\Sigma_{\text{rt}} = 
\begin{pmatrix}
\sigma_r^2 & 0 \\
0 & \sigma_t^2
\end{pmatrix}
\]
in the local radial–tangential coordinate frame. This anisotropic model reflects the idea that measurement uncertainty is typically larger in the direction \textit{around} the ring (tangential) than \textit{towards/away from} the centre (radial), especially when holes are arc-fitted or distorted by curvature.

\section*{Model Comparison}

To formally assess whether the data favour the anisotropic model, we used the Savage-Dickey density ratio to compute the Bayes factor between the isotropic and anisotropic models. This method is valid when one model (here, the isotropic model) is a special case of the other (the anisotropic model), obtained by setting a specific parameter to a fixed value.

In our case, the isotropic model corresponds to $\Delta = \sigma_t - \sigma_r = 0$, where the radial and tangential uncertainties are equal. The Savage-Dickey ratio compares the posterior and prior densities of this parameter at $\Delta = 0$:
\[
\text{Bayes Factor} = \frac{p(\Delta = 0 \mid D)}{p(\Delta = 0)}.
\]

We estimated these densities using kernel density estimation (KDE) applied to posterior and prior samples of $\Delta$. The posterior placed almost no probability mass at $\Delta = 0$, whereas the prior allowed moderate support:
\[
\text{Posterior density at } \Delta = 0: \quad 0.00000, \quad \text{Prior: } 0.00868,
\]
\[
\Rightarrow \text{Bayes Factor (isotropic vs. anisotropic)} \approx 0.000.
\]

This result provides decisive evidence \textit{against} the isotropic model. The data strongly favour a model where radial and tangential uncertainties differ — a physically reasonable outcome, given the geometry of the circular ring. The anisotropic model better captures the direction-dependent structure of measurement error.

\subsection*{Nested Sampling and Log-Evidence Comparison}

To further assess model preference, we used \textit{nested sampling} to estimate the marginal likelihood (also called the \textit{evidence}) for each model. Unlike the Savage-Dickey ratio, which only compares posterior and prior densities at a single point, nested sampling provides a global measure of how well a model explains the data across its entire parameter space.

The evidence for a model $\mathcal{M}$ is defined as:
\[
\log Z = \log \int \mathcal{L}(\theta) \, \pi(\theta) \, d\theta,
\]
where $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ is the likelihood and $\pi(\theta)$ is the prior. A higher evidence indicates a better balance between goodness-of-fit and model complexity.

Nested sampling proceeds by drawing a set of live points from the prior and iteratively replacing the worst-fitting point with a better one, gradually focusing on regions of high likelihood. This allows both the evidence and the posterior to be estimated simultaneously.

In our implementation, we used the \texttt{dynesty} library with 500 live points and the `rwalk` sampling method. The log-likelihood function was compiled using \texttt{jax.jit} for speed, and the prior transform was matched to the priors used in our Bayesian inference (e.g., normal priors for $N$ and $R$, log-normal priors for $\sigma_r$ and $\sigma_t$).

The resulting log-evidence estimates were:
\[
\log Z_{\text{aniso}} = 126.13 \pm 0.70, \quad \log Z_{\text{iso}} = -141.80 \pm 0.71.
\]
The difference in log-evidence,
\[
\Delta \log Z = \log Z_{\text{aniso}} - \log Z_{\text{iso}} \approx 267.93,
\]
is overwhelmingly in favour of the anisotropic model. According to standard interpretive thresholds (e.g., Kass and Raftery), a log-evidence difference greater than 10 is considered ``very strong'' evidence — here, the support is decisive.

Together with the Savage-Dickey result, this confirms that the data clearly favour a model that allows for separate treatment of radial and tangential uncertainties.

\section*{Conclusion}

In this project, we applied Bayesian inference techniques to infer the number of holes in the calendar ring of the Antikythera Mechanism, using fragmentary archaeological data. We compared two statistical models: an isotropic Gaussian model assuming equal uncertainty in all directions, and an anisotropic model that distinguishes between radial and tangential uncertainties.

Both models were implemented using JAX, with gradients verified via finite differences and maximum likelihood solutions obtained through L-BFGS-B optimisation. Bayesian posterior sampling was conducted using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, and model comparison was carried out using both the Savage-Dickey density ratio and nested sampling for evidence estimation.

The anisotropic model consistently outperformed the isotropic model. Visually, it provided more accurate posterior predictive distributions for individual hole positions, capturing elliptical error structures consistent with physical intuition. Quantitatively, the Savage-Dickey ratio yielded a Bayes factor near zero in favour of the isotropic model, indicating negligible posterior mass at the isotropic subspace. Furthermore, nested sampling revealed a substantial log-evidence difference of $\Delta \log Z \approx 268$ in favour of the anisotropic model — decisively confirming its superiority.

Beyond statistical performance, the anisotropic model offers a more realistic representation of the measurement process: while radial positions are relatively well constrained by the curvature of the ring, tangential positions are more susceptible to misalignment and fragment distortion. This physical insight is reflected in the recovered posterior: $\sigma_r < \sigma_t$ with high confidence.

Taken together, our findings support a total hole count of approximately 355, consistent with a 354-day lunar calendar, and provide strong evidence that the measurement errors are direction-dependent. This highlights how carefully constructed statistical models — grounded in physical assumptions and evaluated through principled Bayesian methods — can yield valuable insights even from incomplete archaeological records.
